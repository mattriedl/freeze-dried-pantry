Freeze dried dog treats are easy to make at home: cook plain, lean meat like chicken breast, cut it into small pieces, spread it on trays, and run a normal freeze drying cycle until every piece is crisp and dry all the way through. You can also freeze dry dog-safe fruits and veggies like blueberries, banana and cooked sweet potato. Most batches take roughly 20 to 40 hours, depending on the food, your machine and the humidity in your room.

Homemade treats give you full control over what your dog eats: one ingredient, no fillers, no seasoning. Below you'll learn how to freeze dry dog treats safely, from picking foods to sealing the finished batch.

## Why make freeze dried dog treats at home

Store-bought freeze dried dog treats are popular because dogs love them and the labels are short. They also cost a lot for how little you get, since you're paying for a light bag with the water already removed.

If you own a home freeze dryer, making your own has some clear perks:

- **You know exactly what's inside.** Plain chicken is just plain chicken.
- **You can buy in bulk.** A family pack of chicken breast can become weeks of treats.
- **You choose the size.** Tiny cubes for training, bigger pieces for chewing.
- **They store well.** Properly dried and sealed treats keep far longer than fresh meat.

The tradeoff is machine time. A batch can tie up your freeze dryer for a day or more, so many people fill one tray with dog treats and the rest with food for the family.

New to the process? Our [beginner's guide to freeze drying food at home](/how-to-freeze-dry-food/) covers the basics, and they apply to pet treats too.

## Best foods for freeze dried dog treats

Lean protein is the classic choice. Lean foods dry more evenly and store longer than fatty ones. Many dog-safe fruits and vegetables also freeze dry well and make lighter, lower-calorie snacks.

| Food | Prep | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Chicken breast | Cook plain, cut into 1/4 to 1/2 inch cubes or thin strips | The go-to for freeze dried chicken dog treats |
| Turkey breast | Cook plain, cube or slice thin | Very lean, stores well |
| Lean beef | Cook plain, trim fat, cube | Trim as much visible fat as you can |
| Chicken hearts | Cook, slice in halves or quarters | Rich and chewy before drying, crunchy after |
| Beef or chicken liver | Cook, cut small | Very rich in vitamin A; feed sparingly |
| White fish or salmon | Cook plain, remove all bones, cube | Fattier fish keeps for a shorter time |
| Sweet potato or pumpkin | Cook plain, cube or spoon plain puree into small dots | Skip pie filling, which has sugar and spices |
| Blueberries | Pierce or halve each berry | Whole berries dry slowly without a slit |
| Banana | Slice about 1/4 inch thick | High in sugar, so keep portions small |
| Apple | Core, remove all seeds, slice thin | Seeds and core don't belong in treats |
| Carrots or green beans | Blanch, then slice or cut small | Crunchy, low-calorie options |

### Foods to keep out of dog treats

Some common foods are toxic to dogs or simply poor treat choices. Leave these out:

- Grapes and raisins
- Onion, garlic, chives and leeks, including powders and seasoning blends
- Chocolate and cocoa
- Xylitol (also called birch sugar), found in some peanut butters, yogurts and sugar-free products
- Macadamia nuts
- Cooked bones of any kind, which can splinter
- Salty, smoked or cured meats like bacon, jerky, deli meat and sausage
- Seasoned leftovers, gravies and marinades

Also keep your own sweet freeze dried snacks away from the dog. Our [guide to freeze drying candy](/how-to-freeze-dry-candy/) is for people only.

## Raw vs cooked: food safety first

This is the most important part of making freeze dried dog treats.

**Freeze drying does not kill pathogens.** It removes water, but bacteria like Salmonella and Listeria can survive the process and become active again when the food picks up moisture. The FDA has issued many recalls of commercial raw pet foods for these bacteria. Contaminated treats can make dogs sick, and the germs can also spread to people who handle the treats, clean up after the dog, or let it lick their faces.

For homemade treats, the safer approach is simple:

- **Cook meat, poultry and fish first.** Use the same safe internal temperatures you'd use for your own food, such as 165°F for poultry and 160°F for ground meat.
- **Cool cooked food quickly.** Get it into the fridge, freezer or freeze dryer promptly.
- **Keep raw prep clean.** Wash your hands, cutting boards, knives and trays after handling raw meat.
- **Wash hands after handling the finished treats,** especially if kids help hand them out.

Commercial raw freeze dried treats are often made with safety steps a home kitchen can't easily copy, such as high-pressure processing. If you prefer raw feeding, talk with your veterinarian and buy from a maker that uses a pathogen-reduction step instead of trying to replicate it at home.

## How to freeze dry dog treats step by step

The process is the same as freeze drying any other food. The main difference is keeping everything plain.

### 1. Cook and cool

Cook meat by baking, poaching or boiling with no salt, butter, oil or seasoning. Boiling or poaching adds no fat, which helps shelf life. Trim visible fat and skin, then cool the meat completely in the fridge.

For vegetables like sweet potato, carrots and green beans, cook or blanch them in plain water. Most fruits can go on the trays raw after washing.

### 2. Cut to an even size

Even pieces dry evenly. Aim for:

- **Training treats:** about 1/4 inch cubes
- **Everyday treats:** 1/2 inch cubes or strips about 1/4 inch thick
- **Fruit and veggie slices:** about 1/4 inch thick

Thicker pieces take longer and are more likely to hold a wet center. Match the treat size to your dog, too. Very small dogs need small pieces, and big dogs can gulp tiny cubes without chewing.

### 3. Load the trays

Line trays with parchment or silicone mats and spread food in a single layer with a little space between pieces. Don't pile treats on top of each other.

Fill trays to a moderate depth rather than to the rim. Overloaded trays are one of the most common reasons a batch finishes wet in the middle.

### 4. Pre-freeze (optional but helpful)

Many people pre-freeze loaded trays in a home freezer for several hours or overnight. This can shorten machine time and helps purees hold their shape. If you skip it, your freeze dryer will handle the freezing step for you.

### 5. Run the cycle

Use your machine's standard setting for meats or non-liquid foods unless your manufacturer recommends otherwise. Follow your freeze dryer's manual, since settings and menus vary by brand and model.

### 6. Check for dryness

When the cycle ends, break open a few of the thickest pieces. Each one should be:

- Crisp and light, with no cool or damp feel in the center
- Evenly colored inside, with no darker, glossy core
- Able to snap or crumble rather than bend

If any piece seems damp, add more dry time and check again. It's much better to over-dry a little than to store treats with leftover moisture.

## Cycle times and settings

Times vary a lot with your machine, how full the trays are, piece size, fat content and room humidity. Treat these as rough ranges, not exact targets:

- **Cooked lean chicken or turkey cubes:** about 20 to 30 hours
- **Cooked beef, hearts or liver:** about 24 to 36 hours
- **Cooked fish:** about 24 to 36 hours
- **Fruit slices and blueberries:** about 24 to 40 hours, since sugary fruit is slower
- **Sweet potato or pumpkin dots:** about 24 to 36 hours

Warm, humid rooms usually add time. Many home freeze dryers run more efficiently in a cool space with good airflow around the unit.

Meat and fruit can share a batch, but the whole run lasts as long as the slowest tray takes. If you're short on time and only need a few chewy snacks, a dehydrator is another option. Our [freeze dryer vs dehydrator comparison](/freeze-dryer-vs-dehydrator/) explains how the two methods differ.

## How to store freeze dried dog treats

Freeze dried food stays crisp only as long as it stays dry. Package your treats soon after the cycle ends, before they pull moisture from the air.

### Short-term storage

For treats you'll use within a few weeks to a couple of months:

- Use a glass jar or airtight container with a tight lid
- Keep it in a cool, dark cupboard
- Close it right after each use
- Add a food-safe desiccant pack if your home is humid

### Long-term storage

For longer storage, seal treats in Mylar bags with an oxygen absorber sized for the bag. Store the bags somewhere cool, dark and dry. Lean meat and plain produce usually keep much longer this way than in a jar you open every day.

Fattier foods like salmon, darker meat and organ meats can go stale or rancid sooner, so plan to use those first.

### Signs a batch has gone bad

Throw treats out if they:

- Turn soft, chewy or sticky
- Smell sour, rancid or off
- Show any mold or discoloration

Label every bag or jar with the food and the date so you know what to use first.

## Feeding tips for dogs

Freeze dried treats are concentrated. A small cube of chicken weighs very little but still carries the calories of the fresh meat.

- **Follow the 10% rule.** A common vet guideline is to keep treats to about 10% of your dog's daily calories.
- **Introduce new foods slowly.** Start with a few pieces to see how your dog's stomach handles them.
- **Go easy on rich foods.** Fatty meats and organ meats can upset some dogs' stomachs.
- **Keep water nearby.** Dry treats can make dogs thirsty.
- **Supervise chewing.** Watch for gulping, especially with eager eaters.
- **Talk to your vet** before adding treats if your dog has allergies, a medical condition or a prescription diet.

Freeze dried eggs are another option some owners use as a topper. If you want to try that, our guide on [how to freeze dry eggs](/how-to-freeze-dry-eggs/) covers safe handling. Serve eggs to dogs plain and fully cooked.

## Common mistakes to avoid

Most problems with homemade freeze dried dog treats come down to a few avoidable mistakes:

- **Using seasoned or leftover meat.** Onion and garlic hide in many seasonings and broths.
- **Skipping the cooking step.** Freeze drying won't make raw meat safe.
- **Leaving fat and skin on.** Fat slows drying and shortens shelf life.
- **Cutting uneven pieces.** Big chunks stay wet while small ones finish.
- **Overloading trays.** Crowded trays dry unevenly and take longer.
- **Not checking the center.** A crisp outside can hide a damp middle.
- **Storing too soon or in a leaky bag.** Moisture brings back softness and spoilage.

## Homemade vs store-bought freeze dried dog treats

Store-bought treats are convenient and come in shelf-stable packs. Many brands use single ingredients, and some raw products use safety steps that home kitchens don't have. They're also pricey per ounce, and prices change often, so compare current options before you buy.

Homemade treats cost mostly what you pay for the meat or produce, plus machine time and packaging. You control sourcing, size and ingredients. The catch is that you need a freeze dryer and the patience for a long cycle.
