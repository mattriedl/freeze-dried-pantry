Freeze dried cat treats are easy to make at home: cut plain, cooked meat or fish into small pieces, spread them on trays, and run a normal freeze drying cycle until every piece is crisp and dry all the way through. Chicken breast, turkey, salmon, shrimp and small minnows are popular picks. Most loads take roughly 20 to 40 hours, depending on the food, your machine and your room's humidity.

Homemade treats let you control exactly what goes in: one ingredient, no fillers, no seasoning. Here's how to choose the right foods, prep them safely, run the cycle and store the results.

## Why make freeze dried cat treats at home

Store-bought freeze dried cat treats are usually just one or two ingredients, and cats tend to love them. They're also expensive for how much you get, since you're paying for a small bag of very light food.

If you already own a home freeze dryer, making your own has a few clear benefits:

- **You know the ingredient list.** Plain chicken is plain chicken.
- **You can use what you have.** Trimmed chicken breast or a fillet of salmon can become several weeks of treats.
- **Pieces come out the size you want.** Small cubes work well for training or puzzle feeders.
- **Long storage.** Properly dried and sealed treats keep far longer than fresh meat.

The tradeoff is time. A batch ties up your machine for a day or more, so many people run a tray of cat treats alongside other foods with similar drying needs.

If you're new to the process, read our [beginner's guide to freeze drying food at home](/how-to-freeze-dry-food/) first. The basics are the same for pet treats.

## Best foods for freeze dried cat treats

Cats are obligate carnivores, so the best treats are plain animal protein. Lean foods dry more evenly and store longer than fatty ones.

| Food | Prep | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Chicken breast | Cook plain, cut into 1/4 to 1/2 inch cubes | Lean, cheap and a favorite with most cats |
| Turkey breast | Cook plain, cube or shred | Very lean, stores well |
| Chicken hearts | Cook, slice in half or quarters | Rich and chewy before drying; crunchy after |
| Salmon or white fish | Cook plain, remove all bones, flake or cube | Fattier fish stores for a shorter time |
| Small minnows | Use food-grade fish, cook, leave whole | Popular as a whole-fish treat |
| Shrimp | Cook plain, peel, chop | Remove shells and tails |
| Beef or chicken liver | Cook, cut very small | Feed sparingly; liver is very rich in vitamin A |

A few foods to avoid entirely:

- Anything seasoned, salted, smoked or marinated
- Onion, garlic, chives and leeks, including powders, which are toxic to cats
- Cooked bones of any kind, which can splinter
- Deli meats, sausages and other processed meats high in salt
- Grapes, raisins, chocolate and other people treats
- Dairy-heavy items like freeze dried cheese or milk, which many adult cats don't digest well

Also skip sweet freeze dried snacks. Our [guide to freeze drying candy](/how-to-freeze-dry-candy/) is for people, not pets.

## Raw vs cooked: the food safety rules

This is the most important part of making freeze dried cat treats at home.

**Freeze drying does not kill pathogens.** It removes water, but bacteria such as Salmonella and Listeria can survive the process and come back to life when the food picks up moisture, including in your cat's mouth. The FDA has issued many recalls of commercial raw pet foods for these bacteria. Health agencies have also linked bird flu (H5N1) infections in cats to raw pet food and raw milk.

For homemade treats, the safer approach is simple:

- **Cook meat, poultry, fish and shrimp first.** Cook to the same safe internal temperatures you'd use for your own food, such as 165°F for poultry.
- **Cook fish before freezing it.** Raw fish contains an enzyme called thiaminase that breaks down thiamine (vitamin B1), and cats need plenty of thiamine. Cooking destroys the enzyme.
- **Cool cooked food quickly** and get it into the freezer or freeze dryer promptly.
- **Wash your hands, boards and trays** after handling raw meat, just as you would when cooking for your family.

Commercial raw freeze dried treats are made with controls that a home kitchen doesn't have. If you prefer raw feeding, talk with your veterinarian and buy from a maker that uses a pathogen-reduction step rather than trying to replicate it at home.

## How to make freeze dried cat treats step by step

### 1. Cook the protein plainly

Bake, boil, poach or steam the meat or fish with no oil, butter, salt or seasoning. Poaching or boiling is easy and keeps the meat moist. Pour off and discard any fat that renders out.

### 2. Trim and cut into small pieces

Remove skin, visible fat, gristle and every bone you can find. Cut pieces to about 1/4 to 1/2 inch thick. Smaller, even pieces dry faster and more evenly, and they're the right size for a cat's mouth. Shredded meat also works well and dries quickly.

### 3. Load the trays

Line trays with parchment or silicone mats so pieces don't stick. Spread the food in a single layer with a little space between pieces. Don't pile food up, because thick spots are where moisture hides.

As a rough guide, keep the layer no more than about half an inch deep. You can usually fit one to two pounds of cooked, cubed meat on a medium home freeze dryer tray, but check your machine's manual for its tray limits.

### 4. Pre-freeze (optional but helpful)

Freezing the loaded trays solid in your home freezer first can shorten the cycle. Many home units can also freeze the food themselves, so follow your manufacturer's guidance.

### 5. Run the cycle

Start a standard cycle. Lean cooked chicken and turkey often finish in roughly 20 to 30 hours. Fish, hearts and liver may take longer, sometimes 30 to 40 hours or more. Times vary a lot by machine, how full the trays are, the food's moisture and the temperature and humidity of the room.

Adding a few hours of extra dry time is a cheap way to make sure treats are fully done.

### 6. Check for dryness

When the cycle ends, break open the thickest pieces from several spots on each tray. They should be:

- Light and crisp or firm all the way to the center
- Room temperature, not cold to the touch
- Free of any dark, dense or damp-looking core

If any piece feels cool, soft or chewy inside, run more dry time before you package anything.

## Can you freeze dry cat food?

Yes, you can freeze dry cat food, including plain cooked meat and canned wet cat food. Wet food is usually spread thinly on lined trays, dried until crumbly, and then broken into bits. Some owners crumble it over regular meals as a topper.

Keep a few things in mind:

- **Use homemade results as treats or toppers.** A complete diet needs the right balance of nutrients, including taurine. Don't switch your cat to a homemade diet without guidance from a veterinarian.
- **Handle it like fresh food.** Start with unspoiled food, and don't leave opened wet food sitting out before it goes in the freezer.
- **Rehydrate only what you'll serve.** Rehydrated food is as perishable as fresh wet food, so refrigerate leftovers and toss them after a day or two.

If you have a dog too, the same method works for plain dog treats, but check species-specific safety for each food.

## Freeze dried minnows for cats

Freeze dried minnows for cats are one of the most popular store-bought whole-fish treats, and many cats go wild for the crunch. You can make them at home, with a few extra precautions:

- **Buy food-grade fish.** Use small fish sold for human consumption, such as smelt or similar small whole fish. Don't use live bait or feeder fish, which aren't raised or handled as food.
- **Cook them first.** Steam or poach them plain, which also deals with the thiaminase issue in raw fish.
- **Keep them small.** Tiny whole fish have soft, small bones that cats typically eat in commercial minnow treats. Avoid larger fish with big, sharp bones.
- **Expect a longer dry time** than lean chicken, and check the thickest part of each fish for dryness.

Fish is higher in fat than chicken breast, so whole-fish treats don't store as long. Make smaller batches and use them sooner.

## How to store freeze dried cat treats

Freeze dried food pulls moisture from the air fast, so package treats as soon as the cycle is done.

- **Short term (daily use):** An airtight glass jar or zip-top bag in a cool, dry cupboard. Plan to use the treats within a few weeks to a couple of months, and close the lid quickly each time.
- **Long term:** Mylar bags with an oxygen absorber, heat sealed, stored somewhere cool and dark. Lean meat treats stored this way can last a long time; fattier fish has a shorter shelf life because fat can go rancid.
- **Label everything** with the food and date.

Throw out any treats that smell off, look moldy, or have turned soft and chewy. That means moisture got in.

For a bigger-picture comparison of storage life, see our breakdown of a [freeze dryer vs a dehydrator](/freeze-dryer-vs-dehydrator/). Dehydrated jerky-style treats are another option but don't keep as long.

## How to serve freeze dried treats to your cat

Most cats happily eat freeze dried treats dry, straight from the jar. You can also crumble them over food or soak them in a little water for a softer treat, which may help older cats or cats who don't drink much.

Treats should stay a small part of the diet. A common veterinary guideline is that treats make up no more than about 10% of a cat's daily calories. Freeze dried meat is calorie-dense for its size, so a few small pieces a day is plenty for most cats.

When introducing a new treat, offer one or two pieces and watch for vomiting, diarrhea or other changes. Check with your vet before adding treats if your cat has kidney disease, food allergies, diabetes, a weight problem or is on a prescription diet.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Using seasoned leftovers.** Rotisserie chicken and other people food often contain salt, onion and garlic.
- **Freeze drying raw meat and assuming it's safe.** Freeze drying is not a kill step.
- **Cutting pieces too big.** Thick chunks often stay moist in the middle.
- **Packing trays too full.** Crowded trays dry unevenly and stretch the cycle.
- **Packaging too soon.** Food that's still cool or soft in the center will spoil.
- **Leaving bones in fish or poultry.** Debone carefully unless you're using tiny whole fish.
- **Overfeeding.** Even healthy treats add up quickly for a small animal.

If some pieces keep coming out chewy, it usually means they were too thick, the trays were overloaded, or the room was warm and humid. Cut smaller, load lighter, and add extra dry time.
